A special thank you to the developers who helped bring our Developer Spotlight to Haven West Midlands!

As part of the event, we invited industry professionals to share their experiences and insights through recorded videos for our participants to watch throughout the day.

A huge thank you to:

## Reu Wilde, Jinx Gaming

<figure class="post-figure">
  {% include picture.html photo="haven-spotlight-reu-wilde" sizes="(max-width: 960px) 100vw, 900px" %}
</figure>

Reu, the founder of Jinx Gaming, walked attendees through the first step the industry takes with any new game: the game design document. He shared a one-page “white paper GDD” that we printed for every team, covering the game’s name, theme, target platforms, key mechanics, characters, environments and story beats.

> That, first and foremost, is where you start making a game. Not in a game engine, not with drawing, but putting pen to paper and getting your ideas down.
{: .post-quote}

He also told the story of his own first game jam, run by the studio behind the BAFTA-winning *OlliOlli*. As a team of two with no time for polished art, they photographed paper drawings and turned them into a comic-book collage for a speedrunning skateboarding worm, and being the smallest, most resourceful team got them noticed.

> Don’t be afraid to get a little weird. Sometimes weird is good, especially if you want to be creative and push those boundaries.
{: .post-quote}

His parting advice suited a 12-hour jam perfectly:

> Don’t worry so much about finishing it. Just worry about getting stuck in and doing the best that you can.
{: .post-quote}

## Kyle Davies, Apocalypse Games

<figure class="post-figure">
  {% include picture.html photo="haven-spotlight-kyle-davies" sizes="(max-width: 960px) 100vw, 900px" %}
</figure>

Kyle is one of the directors of Apocalypse Games, where he writes the game design documents and leads programming. He started young: experimenting with Roblox Studio at six, then Kodu Game Lab and Scratch, while teaching himself C++ at school. For his master’s dissertation he built a Slender-style horror game, testing how different enemy behaviours change how frightening a game feels.

His advice for a first project was to keep it small:

> Working on a smaller project with a single mechanic or a concept allows you to reach your goal and have people actually play those projects once you publish them.
{: .post-quote}

He also had a good word for game jams like Haven:

> Despite the large workload of making our projects at Apocalypse Games, I still make sure to have time to join at least two game jams per year, because I believe they provide huge value no matter where you are on your development journey.
{: .post-quote}

And he cleared up a common misconception about the job:

> A significant portion of your time is spent with the code editor open, not actually playing games, writing code for several hours per day.
{: .post-quote}

## Elliot Spencer, Apocalypse Games

<figure class="post-figure">
  {% include picture.html photo="haven-spotlight-elliot-spencer" sizes="(max-width: 960px) 100vw, 900px" %}
</figure>

Elliot, also a director at Apocalypse Games, started out at 17 building small Unity mini-games, and carried that through five to six years of university to running his own studio after winning a grant. Today he looks after the company’s admin, finance and partnerships. One of his favourite projects was a university tower defence game, built while learning how to make AI enemies find their way around a map.

He stressed keeping your skills sharp:

> If you do not keep up with it, you can lose that skill quite quickly. If you don’t keep sharpening the pencil.
{: .post-quote}

He warned against scope creep, piling every idea into a game without deciding what it actually needs, and echoed Reu’s point about planning before you build:

> Being able to plan and being able to focus down what it is you want in your game is probably the most important thing.
{: .post-quote}

He also encouraged attendees to get comfortable reaching out to people:

> Send them a message, say hey, and have the confidence to be able to begin a conversation. It’s very difficult to do for some people, but it’s a skill that you definitely learn over time.
{: .post-quote}
